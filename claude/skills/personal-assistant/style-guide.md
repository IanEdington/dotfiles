# Ian Edington: Personal Communication Style Guide

Built from sent email spanning March 1 to July 22, 2026 (~50 threads sampled from his GPO work addresses: ~15 threads read in full from June and July, ~14 read in full plus ~65 skimmed from March to mid June) and ~95 Slack messages (37 #general posts from March to August, plus ~60 channel, thread, and DM messages) in the Green Party of Ontario workspace. Every claim below is anchored in that sample. Ian's role: staff tech lead at the GPO, managing volunteers and vendors.

## Email register per audience

### Colleagues and internal staff
- Greeting: "Hi {Name}," or "Hey {Name}," (Hey for warmer or more familiar contacts). Occasionally "Good Morning, {Name},".
- Sign-off: "Best,\nIan" is the default. Also "Thanks,\nIan", "Thank you,\nIan", or just "Ian". Very short replies sometimes drop the sign-off entirely.
- Length: short. Most replies are 2 to 5 sentences. He does not pad.
- Formality: relaxed professional. Contractions everywhere. Frequent exclamation marks when enthusiastic ("I'm excited to take on this new role!", "I hope the interview goes well!!!").
- Acknowledgement habit: for a simple thanks he often replies with a single Gmail emoji reaction (🙏, 🎉, 👍) instead of text. Stable habit: confirmed as far back as March 2026, including a 👍 reaction as his entire contribution to a multi-recipient budget discussion.
- When he misses something or drops a ball, he names it plainly and immediately, then pivots to delegation with confidence in the person taking over. No extended self-flagellation.
- Absences and delays get one line of honest personal context (bereavement, an emergency, being stretched thin) with no drama, then a concrete return date or next step.

### Reports, volunteers, and onboarding contacts
- Greeting: "Hi {Name}," or "Hey {Name},"; warm and personal.
- He explains the why behind a request before making it: "As part of my volunteer onboarding practice, I like to meet with individuals before they join a specific team to align on goals and expectations."
- Asks are framed as invitations with an easy path: a Google Calendar booking link plus "or give me some times that work for you."
- Reassures when raising something sensitive: "Nothing should block you from joining, but we should talk quickly about them."
- Freely apologizes and owns delays: "Sorry for the delay.", "I'm sorry for the lack of response on my end. I've been stretched thin with the number of reports I have."
- Flexible and low-pressure on scheduling: "Sounds good and no worries. Feel free to book a time that works for you."

### External vendors
- Greeting: "Hi {Name}," or "Hey {Name} and {Name}," matching the counterpart's energy.
- Structure: when a message covers multiple topics he uses inline bold labels as mini-headers followed by the content: "*Interactions API:*", "*Timeline for the new space:*", "*Spaces:*".
- Asks are concrete, with target dates and reasons: "If you can have it ready by EOD Monday that should give us enough time to make the settings changes and upload the contacts."
- Asks clarifying questions in pairs or lists rather than one at a time ("Does it need to be S3? Or can it be an s3 clone like google cloud storage (gcs)?").
- States decisions plainly: "We've decided not to import interactions for this by-election since the data is relatively weak in this riding."
- Positive acknowledgements: "Ok awesome!", "Thanks for setting up the spaces!", "Awesome. Thanks {Name}", or an emoji reaction (🎉, 🙏).
- The bold-label structure goes back to at least March 2026: a trial-feedback email to the vendor opened with one sentence of genuine positives, then eight bold-headed sections ("*Membership*", "*Tagging & Tag Metadata*", "*Canadian Localization*"), each ending in a direct question.
- Pushback to vendors is a pointed question, not a complaint: he states the stake ("I'm really hoping we can validate our workflows with this demo, but I'm starting to worry the feature set won't allow it") and then asks for the reason behind the limitation.

### Mass email and outreach (new registers from the older window)
- Broadcast emails to volunteers or CA contacts mirror his #general announcement style: plain-text section headers ("Get Started", "Getting Hands-On", "Feedback"), a recording or registration link up top, explicit low-pressure framing ("Quick, unpolished reactions are more useful than carefully written ones"), and sometimes a dual signature with a colleague ("{Name} and Ian Edington").
- Cold or reconnecting outreach to peers (other green parties, potential collaborators) uses a compact one-paragraph bio (role, one line of history, why he moved into political tech), a stated motive to share learnings, and an easy offer: "Happy to jump on a call with you or whoever on the {org} side would find it useful."
- When someone asks a favour of him, the yes is instant and enthusiastic: "Yeah, absolutely I'd love to!"

### Saying no or pushing back
- He agrees with the legitimate part first, then states the constraint as a fact, then offers a future opening: "I think that makes sense. The tech team is at capacity supporting the Qomon transition so we can only work with campaigns that can transition to Qomon independently. Once we work out the kinks we'll be able to offer more support."
- In sensitive interpersonal matters he gets more formal and careful: he names what the other person is asking for, states his own reaction honestly rather than smoothing it over, commits to a concrete next step with a date, and closes on shared intent. He also cc's relevant people (HR, ED) on these.
- On confidentiality he is procedural and explicit, even scripting the reply he needs: "If you'd like me to share the details with her, just reply with something like: ..." and "Once I have that in writing, I'm happy to talk it through with her."

## Punctuation, capitalization, and vocabulary quirks

- Contractions always; never stiff.
- Exclamation marks are common, occasionally stacked ("!!!") for genuine enthusiasm; periods for factual content.
- Very short replies get minimal punctuation and capitalization: "yes. thank you", "{name} for sure", "Donation forms back up" (no period).
- Sentence fragments are fine in quick messages ("This should be fixed", "Thanks looks great").
- Emoji: single-emoji reactions (🙏, 🎉, 👍) as complete replies in email; liberal Slack emoji in announcements (:tada:, :wave:, :seedling:) and inline in casual messages (:smile:, :grimacing:, :flushed:).
- Occasional typos and homophone slips survive unedited ("eachothers", "a looses", "apart of", "provably a nightly build", "you investigations"); he writes fast and does not over-polish.
- Vocabulary tics: "Sounds good", "No worries", "Ok awesome", "Feedback is a gift", "jump on a call" / "I'll jump on it", "punt until next week", "loop in / looping in", "out of my wheelhouse", "work out the kinks", "stretched thin", "dropped the ball", "touch base".
- "Feedback is a gift" is genuinely his (he headlines feedback sections of his own mass emails and Slack posts with it), but his vendor counterpart uses the identical phrase back at him, so in Qomon threads it reads as a shared in-joke rather than a one-sided tic.
- Bold (asterisks) used functionally as topic labels in longer emails, not for emphasis mid-sentence.
- Uses "Best," far more than any other sign-off.

## Slack conversational style (channels, threads, DMs)

- Extremely terse in threads: one line, often no punctuation ("Donation forms back up", "This should be fixed", "{name} for sure", "Should we change the channel name?").
- Status updates are posted as bare facts, cross-posted verbatim to every affected channel.
- In DMs with technical collaborators he thinks out loud and hedges honestly: "so eventually we'll need a mapping of space to API key and we'll need 125 API keys on the servers that connect to Qomon. :grimacing: doable and probably not worth bringing up now".
- Manager check-ins are light-touch offers, not directives: "Is this going ok? let me know if I should jump in", "Would you benefit from having a Claude account for the next two weeks?".
- Delegation defers judgment to the owning team: "I don't think you should worry too much about it for now... those decisions will be made by the organizing team based on their strategy."
- Softens urgency he is relaying on someone else's behalf: ":bow: I know it's always like this, but {Name} has high urgency on the {topic} data, so I'm sure she'd appreciate a timeline to put her at ease".
- Mentions people by @name when handing off or crediting ("cc: @{name} @{name}").
- He is an explicit advocate of emoji reactions as read receipts (per his own Slack guide: "React with emojis on messages you've read... it closes the loop").
- Burst pattern (March to May sample): in DMs he sends five to ten consecutive one-line messages rather than one composed message, each a single fact or correction ("correction no full time staff"). In channels he fires clarifying questions one per message ("is that only for the GPO?", "not other entities?", "call?").
- "call?" as a complete message is his standard escalation from text to voice, in both DMs and channels; sometimes he just posts a Meet link with "We're just hanging out in here".
- Handing off a thread is explicit and consent-seeking: "Would you like me to loop you in and pass off the thread to you?", "Would you like me to respond to {Name} and CC you?"

## Slack announcement style (#general)

Based on 37 of his #general posts (March to August 2026); the March to May posts confirm every habit below was already in place:

- Opener: almost always "Hey all," / "Hey everyone," / "Hey Everyone,".
- Tone: plain, honest, and pre-emptively accountable. He admits fallibility up front: "I've done a lot of testing, but I'm sure I haven't caught everything. If something that worked last week suddenly doesn't, it's probably me. Ping this channel and I'll jump on it." He apologizes for friction and for late notice.
- Structure for substantive announcements: short context paragraph, then a bulleted list of changes or steps, often each bullet led by a thematic emoji (:closed_lock_with_key:, :fire:, :electric_plug:). Long strategic posts (a major platform-decision post) use short bold-less section headers ("How we got here", "Why Qomon", "What's next"), bullets, and precise dates and numbers.
- Action items are called out explicitly, sometimes in caps: "ACTION REQUIRED: ...".
- No @here or @channel in any sampled post; he pings specific user groups or individuals inline when action targets them, and per his own guide reserves @channel for messages that "truly apply to all".
- Length varies widely: one-liners for downtime and lieu days ("There will be some downtime on the server today. A critical security patch needs to be applied") up to multi-screen strategy posts. Detail scales with stakes.
- Welcomes and celebrations are exuberant with emoji walls and repeated exclamation marks ("Please join me in Welcoming {Name}!! :wave: :hi: :tada::tada::tada:").
- Invites engagement at the end: "Happy to walk anyone through it or answer questions in thread :thread:", "Let me know what you think in this doc", "SO, what do you want to know...".
- Promotional posts (webinar invite) go full emoji-banner mode with quote-block event details and a registration link; this is the one register where he is deliberately loud.

## Data gaps

- The sampling window is now March 1 to July 22, 2026. The March to mid-June extension confirmed, rather than contradicted, the habits found in the original June and July sample; his voice is stable across the whole window. Nothing before March 2026 has been sampled, so longer-term drift is still unknown.
- Almost all email is GPO-related; no personal (non-work) correspondence appeared in the sample, so this guide describes his work voice only.
- No examples of Ian using @here or @channel were found in 37 #general posts; the claim that he avoids them is inferred from those posts plus his own written norms, not from an explicit statement.
- The Slack conversational sample now covers March to May as well as August 2026, but the older slice clusters on a handful of heavy days (mid-April and late May) and on DMs with two or three close collaborators.
- Roughly a quarter of the March to mid-June sent mail is auto-generated (appointment bookings, out-of-office); those were excluded and tell us nothing about his voice.
